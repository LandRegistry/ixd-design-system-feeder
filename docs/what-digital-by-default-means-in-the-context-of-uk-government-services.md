## Introduction - the reason for the guide

There is an important distinction between **putting an existing government process online and fundamentally rethinking how government delivers a service in a digital world.**

The phrase *digital by default* is particularly susceptible to misunderstanding because people can interpret it through their own experience of government technology: online forms, portals, document uploads, workflow systems and electronic case management.

These can all be useful, but they do not necessarily mean that a service is genuinely digital.

There is also an important distinction between the original UK Government definition of digital by default and the broader, more mature concept of digital transformation reflected in today's Government Digital Standards.

The original 2013 definition was that services should be so straightforward and convenient that people who can use digital services will choose them, while those who cannot are not excluded. The current Government Functional Standard (GovS 005) goes further, emphasising whole-service improvement, user needs, data, integration, accessibility and continuous improvement.

[Government approach to assisted digital](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/government-approach-to-assisted-digital/government-approach-to-assisted-digital)


This is a short, accessible guide aims to do three things:

1. Establishes a common understanding of what digital by default means.
2. Challenges the assumption that digitising existing processes is the same as digital transformation.
3. Gives people practical questions they can use when developing policy, designing services or making operational decisions.

# Digital by default: what does it actually mean for government?

*A short guide to developing a shared understanding of digital government and what it means for the way we design and deliver public services.*

## 1. What do we mean by digital by default?

Digital by default means designing and delivering government services around the needs of the people who use them, making digital the natural, straightforward and most convenient way to access and interact with government wherever that is appropriate.

But there is an important distinction:

**Digital by default does not mean taking an existing paper-based process and putting it on a computer or website. It means rethinking how the service should work in a digital world.**

It is about changing the way government works, not simply changing the technology through which it works.

A genuinely digital service takes advantage of the opportunities that digital technology, data and modern ways of working create to make services simpler, faster, more accessible, more joined-up and more effective.

The objective is not to make an existing process digital. The objective is to deliver a better outcome for the user and government.

## 2. Digitising an existing process is not the same as digital transformation

This is perhaps the most important distinction to understand.

Consider a traditional government application process.

Historically, someone might have:

- Downloaded or requested a paper form.
- Completed the form.
- Gathered supporting documents.
- Posted the application to government.
- Waited for it to be received and processed.
- Been contacted if information was missing.
- Received a letter confirming the outcome.

We can digitise this process relatively easily:

- Put the paper form online.
- Allow someone to type into the form.
- Let them upload supporting documents.
- Send an automated email confirming receipt.
- Give an official an electronic version of the application to process.

This is undoubtedly digitisation. It may improve convenience and efficiency.

But fundamentally, we have preserved the original process. We have simply moved it from paper to a computer.

**Digital transformation asks a different question: why does the process need to work this way at all?**

For example:

- Do we need an application form, or could we already have the information needed to establish eligibility?
- Why are we asking people to provide information government already holds?
- Could we use data to establish eligibility automatically?
- Could we make a decision in real time rather than waiting for someone to process an application?
- Could we notify someone when they need to take action, rather than expecting them to know when to contact us?
- Could we connect different government services so that users do not have to navigate the boundaries between departments?
- Could we prevent a problem arising rather than waiting for someone to apply for help?

The difference is fundamental.

| Digitising an existing process       | Transforming a service                                                     |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Putting a paper form online          | Questioning whether a form is needed                                       |
| Uploading documents                  | Reusing information government already holds, where lawful and appropriate |
| Emailing application updates         | Proactively keeping users informed                                         |
| Automating existing administration   | Removing unnecessary administrative steps                                  |
| Moving paper records into a database | Using connected, reliable data to improve decisions                        |
| Making an existing process faster    | Redesigning the process to remove unnecessary work                         |
| Building an online service           | Creating an end-to-end service that meets a user need                      |

Digitisation can be an important step towards transformation, but it should not be mistaken for the destination.

## 3. What does a genuinely digital government service look like?

There is no single technology or technical solution that makes a service digital.

Instead, genuinely digital services tend to share a number of characteristics.

**1. They start with user needs, not existing processes**

We understand what people are trying to achieve and design the service around that outcome.

We do not simply reproduce the way government has historically organised its work, its legislation or its internal structures.

**2. They solve the whole problem**

We consider the entire journey, from the point someone first needs a service to the point their need has been met.

This includes what happens before, during and after an interaction with government.

Users should not have to understand how government is organised to get something done.

**3. They make things simpler**

We actively remove unnecessary steps, duplication, questions, paperwork and administrative effort.

We challenge established processes rather than assuming they must continue because that is how things have always been done.

**4. They make intelligent use of data**

We use information responsibly to make services more proactive, responsive and efficient.

We avoid repeatedly asking people for information that government already holds and is able to reuse lawfully.

**5. They are connected**

Services should work together rather than operate as isolated departmental systems.

We should make appropriate use of common platforms, shared data, reusable technology and common standards.

**6. They are accessible and inclusive**

Digital should be the easiest and most convenient route for people who can use it.

But digital by default does not mean digital only.

We must recognise that some people cannot access or use digital services independently. Appropriate alternative channels and assisted digital support must be available so that nobody is excluded.

**7. They continually improve**

A digital service is never simply finished.

We use evidence, user feedback and performance data to understand how well it works and continually improve it.

We should be able to identify where people struggle, where processes fail and where further improvements can be made.

## 4. What digital by default does NOT mean

There are several common misconceptions worth challenging.

| Misconception                                                           | What it actually means                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| We have an online form, so the service is digital.                      | An online form is only one possible component of a digital service.                                               |
| We have automated our existing process, so we have transformed it.      | Automation can make a poor process faster without making it better.                                               |
| Digital means putting everything online.                                | Digital means choosing the best way to meet user needs, not forcing everyone into one channel.                    |
| Digital is primarily about technology.                                  | Technology enables change, but the real focus is users, services, processes, data and outcomes.                   |
| We need to replicate the existing business process in a new system.     | We should first question whether the existing process is still necessary or appropriate.                          |
| Digital transformation is the responsibility of the IT or digital team. | It is an organisational responsibility involving policy, operations, service delivery, technology and leadership. |
| A digital service is complete when it goes live.                        | A digital service needs ongoing ownership, measurement and improvement.                                           |

## 5. What does this mean for us as a government department?

It means we need to look beyond the systems and processes we have inherited.

Many of our existing services and operating models were designed for a different era. They were built around paper forms, manual checks, correspondence, separate databases, organisational boundaries and the practical limitations of pre-internet government.

Over time, we have introduced technology to support these ways of working.

That has delivered improvements, but it does not necessarily mean we have fundamentally changed the way we operate.

We should therefore challenge ourselves to think differently.

Rather than asking:

- How do we put this process online?
- How do we automate this existing workflow?
- How do we replace this paper form with an electronic form?
- How do we make our existing system work more efficiently?

We should increasingly ask:

- What is the user actually trying to achieve?
- Why do we need this process in the first place?
- Could we remove some or all of these steps?
- What information do we already have that could be used?
- Could we make this service proactive rather than reactive?
- How could we make the experience simpler and more joined-up?
- What could we do differently if we designed this service from scratch today?
- How could we use digital technology and data to achieve better outcomes?

These are not just questions for digital specialists. They are questions for everyone involved in policy, operational delivery, service design, corporate services and organisational leadership.

## 6. A simple test: are we digitising or transforming?

When considering a new initiative, system, process or service improvement, it is worth asking five questions:

1. **User need:** Are we starting with what the user needs to achieve, or with the process we currently operate?
2. **Simplification:** Are we removing unnecessary steps and complexity, or simply transferring them into a digital environment?
3. **Data:** Are we making better use of information and data, or continuing to ask people and colleagues to provide the same information repeatedly?
4. **End-to-end service:** Are we improving the complete journey, or just one part of an existing process?
5. **Outcomes:** Will this genuinely improve the experience and outcomes for users and government, or will it simply make our existing way of working marginally more efficient?

If we are only changing the channel, interface or technology, we may be digitising.

If we are fundamentally improving how a service works and the outcomes it delivers, we are much closer to genuine digital transformation.

## 7. The bottom line

Digital by default is not a technology programme, a website strategy or an ambition to replace paper with electronic forms.

It is a different way of thinking about government and how we deliver public services.

It means using the opportunities created by digital technology, data and modern ways of working to fundamentally rethink how we design, organise and deliver services.

It means starting with the needs of users, challenging the processes we have inherited, removing unnecessary complexity and continually improving the services we provide.

**The ambition should not be to make our existing processes digital. It should be to make our services work better because we are operating in a digital world.**

That is the difference between having digitised government and becoming a genuinely digital government.

## Further reading: the UK Government's own guidance

These are useful reference points for understanding the principles behind digital government:

1. [Government Functional Standard GovS 005: Digital](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/government-functional-standard-govs-005-digital/government-functional-standard-govs-005-digital-html) – the overarching standard for digital and data activity across government.

4. [Designing good government services: an introduction](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/introduction-designing-government-services) – a useful introduction to thinking about services from beginning to end, front to back and across every channel.
5. [Government approach to assisted digital](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/government-approach-to-assisted-digital/government-approach-to-assisted-digital) – explains why digital by default must not mean excluding people who cannot use digital services independently.

## **Recommendation on how to use this**

Be deliberate about the message put around this guide.

There is a risk that colleagues interpret it as criticism of the work already done to digitise departmental services. That isn't necessary, and it could make people defensive.

Position it as an opportunity to establish a **shared ambition for the next stage of digital maturity**.

One particularly useful distinction to introduce into departmental conversations is:

- **Digitisation:** Making existing processes electronic.
- **Digitalisation:** Using technology to improve existing processes and operations.
- **Digital transformation:** Rethinking and redesigning services, processes and operating models to achieve fundamentally better outcomes.

These terms aren't used consistently across government, but the distinction is helpful.

One point particularly prominent in any internal communications:

> We shouldn't start by asking how we can make an existing process digital. We should start by asking whether that process is still the right way to deliver the outcome we need.

This is the mindset shift worth encouraging across the orgaisation or team.
