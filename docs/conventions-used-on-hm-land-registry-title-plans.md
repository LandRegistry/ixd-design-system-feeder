## About title plans

An HM Land Registry title plan shows the general extent of the land in a registered title.

A title plan should always be read alongside the title register. As well as showing the extent of the registered land, a title plan can use colours, lines, hatching, numbers, letters and other symbols to identify areas referred to in the register.

The meaning of a particular reference depends on the corresponding entry in the register.

The conventions below explain some of the ways information may be represented on a title plan.

## Red edging

Red edging is normally used to show the extent of the registered title.

The red edging will usually follow the inside of a line representing a physical feature, such as a wall, fence or hedge, or another line shown on the Ordnance Survey map.

Title plans normally show general boundaries. The exact legal position of a boundary cannot usually be determined from the position of the red edging alone.

For very small areas of land, where conventional edging would be difficult to show clearly, another method such as tinting may occasionally be used.

## Colour references

Colours may be used to identify particular areas of land referred to in the register.

Some colours have established uses on title plans.

| Convention | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| Red edging | Extent of the registered title |
| Brown tinting or hatching | Land over which the registered title has the benefit of a right of way |
| Blue tinting or hatching | Land subject to a right of way |
| Green edging, tinting or hatching | Land removed from or excluded from the registered title |

A colour should not be interpreted on its own. Its meaning should be confirmed by reading the relevant entry in the title register.

For example, the register might refer to:

> the land tinted brown on the title plan

The title plan provides the geographical reference and the register explains its significance.

## Other colours

Title plans may contain colours other than red, brown, blue and green.

These can be used to distinguish different areas referred to in the register or to reproduce references originating from plans contained in deeds or other documents.

This means that a particular colour does not always have a universal meaning.

Where an older deed describes an area using a particular colour, the title plan may retain that colour where appropriate. However, established title plan conventions may sometimes take precedence.

Always use the title plan together with its register to establish what a coloured area represents.

## Tinting

Tinting uses an area of colour to identify land.

It can be useful where a relatively large area needs to be distinguished from surrounding land.

For example, a title plan may contain an area tinted brown which corresponds with a register entry describing a right benefiting the registered land.

Tinting should allow the underlying map detail to remain understandable.

## Hatching

Hatching uses a pattern of lines rather than a solid area of colour.

It can be used to identify land where another form of reference would be unclear or where different references need to be distinguished.

For example, a title plan might use:

- brown hatching for land over which the registered title has the benefit of a right of way
- blue hatching for land subject to a right of way
- green hatching for land excluded from the registered title

As with tinting, the associated register entry determines the meaning of the reference.

## Coloured edging

A coloured line can be used around an area instead of colouring the whole area.

This can be particularly useful where tinting would make the underlying map less clear or where an area is already affected by another reference.

Different coloured edgings may therefore appear close together on a title plan.

Red edging has a particular significance because it ordinarily identifies the extent of the registered title.

## Numbers and letters

Numbers or letters can be placed on a title plan to identify particular areas.

The register then refers to that identifier.

For example:

> the land numbered 1 on the title plan

Numbers and letters are particularly useful where:

- the area is small
- several areas need to be identified
- using additional colours would make the plan difficult to understand

## More than one reference affecting the same land

Sometimes an area of land is affected by more than one register entry.

Different graphical conventions may be combined to distinguish them. For example, edging might be combined with tinting or hatching.

Where several references appear together, the plan should not be interpreted simply by looking at the colours. The corresponding register entries explain what each reference means.

## Land excluded from a title

An area within the surrounding extent of a title may not form part of the registered land.

Such an area can be shown using green tinting or hatching, together with the surrounding red edging and an appropriate reference in the register.

This is sometimes encountered where an area forms an 'island' within otherwise registered land.

## Lines

Lines can also be used as references.

These may identify a route, feature, boundary or other geographical information referred to in the register.

Different line styles, including broken lines, may be used where this helps distinguish the reference from the underlying Ordnance Survey mapping.

The significance of the line will be explained by the corresponding register entry.

## T and H marks

Some title plans contain T marks or H marks.

These may relate to provisions in deeds or register entries concerning boundaries, walls or fences.

A T or H mark should not by itself be treated as evidence of ownership or responsibility. Its meaning depends on the deed or register entry to which it relates.

## Braces

A brace can be used to show that 2 or more physically separate areas form part of the same plan reference.

This can avoid having to place identical references on every individual part of the land.

The register should be read alongside the plan to understand what that combined reference represents.

## Historic conventions

Older title plans may contain references that are no longer normally added to modern plans.

This can include:

- historic Ordnance Survey field numbers
- different styles of colouring
- older forms of edging or notation

An older plan should not therefore be interpreted solely according to the conventions used on newly created title plans.

The register and any deeds referred to in it may provide important additional information.

## Understanding a title plan

There are 3 particularly important principles when interpreting graphical information on a title plan.

### The plan and register work together

A title plan identifies land graphically. The register explains the significance of many of those graphical references.

Do not interpret a colour, hatch or line without checking the corresponding register entry.

### Colour alone does not always determine meaning

Although some colours have established uses, title plans can contain other colours and references inherited from deeds and earlier plans.

The relevant register entry explains what the plan reference relates to.

### A title plan normally shows general boundaries

The position of edging or another graphical reference on a title plan should not normally be treated as defining the precise legal position of a boundary.

## At-a-glance guide

| What you see | What it commonly indicates |
| --- | --- |
| Red edging | General extent of the registered title |
| Brown tinting or hatching | Benefit of a right of way |
| Blue tinting or hatching | Land subject to a right of way |
| Green edging, tinting or hatching | Land removed from or excluded from the title |
| Other coloured areas | Land referred to by a particular register entry or source document |
| Number or letter | Identifies an area referred to in the register |
| Broken or other line | A linear feature or other geographical reference |
| T or H mark | Reference associated with a boundary, wall, fence or related provision |
| Brace | Separate areas forming a single plan reference |

The title plan and register should always be read together. These conventions help identify the relevant land, but the corresponding register entry explains what the reference means.
